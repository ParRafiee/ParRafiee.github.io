About

Quantitative methods for behavioral and health sciences research.

I'm a Ph.D. candidate in Behavioral Science (Quantitative Methodology) and an M.S. student in Statistics at the University of Rhode Island. I analyze data that change over time and are nested within people, clinics, or days. My work covers longitudinal data analysis (including intensive longitudinal data and randomized controlled trials), multilevel modeling, missing data, and survival analysis in behavioral, clinical, and health research.

I work on both the applied and the methodological side. On the applied side, I help research teams turn their questions into sound analytic plans and carry them through from data cleaning to results. On the methodological side, I use large-scale Monte Carlo simulation to ask a practical question: when can we trust our estimates, and when can't we? All of it runs in reproducible R workflows.

Beyond my own research, I collaborate with clinicians and researchers across institutions, including Brown University Health. I also consult on study design and analysis at URI's Quantitative Consultation Center, and teach statistical programming to undergraduate and graduate students.

Research

What I work on.

Longitudinal Data Analysis

Repeated-measures and panel data, randomized controlled trials, intensive longitudinal and EMA data, growth curve and dynamic SEM models.

Multilevel Modeling

Mixed-effects and hierarchical models for nested data: days within people, patients within clinics, students within schools.

Missing Data Analysis

Full information maximum likelihood, multiple imputation, and Bayesian approaches, and when each one holds up under different missing-data mechanisms.

Survival Analysis

Time-to-event models, including interval-censored outcomes where the exact event time falls between visits.

Structural Equation Modeling

Latent variable models and psychometric evaluation of health and behavioral measures.

Bayesian & Simulation Methods

Bayesian estimation and Monte Carlo studies to evaluate estimator performance under small samples, missing data, and model misspecification.
